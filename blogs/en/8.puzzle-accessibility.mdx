Daily word puzzles look like the most accessible games there are. No reflexes, no timer, no twitch skill, just letters and thinking. Then you look at how the information actually reaches the player and the picture changes.

Wordle tells you everything through colour. Strands hides its answers in a spatial arrangement. Connections asks you to sort by a difficulty scale that is also expressed as colour. All three are, at their core, visual formats carrying non-visual puzzles.

## The Colour Problem

Wordle's feedback is green, yellow and grey. Roughly one man in twelve and one woman in two hundred has some form of colour vision deficiency, and the most common kind makes green and red hard to separate. Green against yellow against grey is not the worst possible combination, but it is far from the safest.

The mitigation is built in: Wordle has a **high contrast mode** in its settings that swaps green and yellow for orange and blue. Blue against orange survives essentially every kind of colour vision deficiency, which is why that pairing turns up so often in accessible design. If you have ever squinted at a tile trying to decide whether it was green, it is worth turning on regardless of whether you consider yourself colour blind.

Connections has a harder version of the same problem. Its four categories run yellow, green, blue, purple, and that order carries meaning: yellow is the most literal group, purple the most wordplay-heavy. A player who cannot cleanly separate those four shades loses not just the grouping but the difficulty signal that tells them how much to distrust an obvious answer. Position on the board does not encode it, so there is no fallback.

## The Grid Problem

Colour is the easier half. The harder half is that two of these puzzles are spatial.

Screen readers move through a page linearly. They are good at text and lists and headings, and they are poor at conveying "these six letters form a path that bends up and to the left". A Wordle row is a straightforward sequence: five letters, five states, read left to right. A Strands board is a 6×8 grid where the answer is a route through it, and reading out 48 letters in order tells you almost nothing about which ones connect.

That is the real accessibility gap in Strands. Not the letters, the geometry. A sighted player scans the board and sees clusters. A player using a screen reader has to hold a 48-letter map in their head and search it mentally.

Connections sits in between. The 16 words are text, so they read out fine, but the board rearranges itself as you solve groups, and keeping track of what moved where is exactly the kind of state change that linear reading handles badly.

## What Each Game Actually Demands

Stripped to the sense it depends on:

- **Wordle** needs you to distinguish three states per letter. The states could be conveyed by sound, by texture, by words. Colour is the implementation, not the requirement.
- **Connections** needs you to hold 16 items and test groupings. The words are text; the difficulty colours are extra information that happens to be colour-only.
- **Strands** needs you to perceive adjacency in two dimensions. This is the one where the visual format is doing genuine work rather than just being a convenient skin.

That ranking is useful if you are making puzzle content, because it tells you where a non-visual version is a small job and where it is a real design problem.

## Making Puzzle Content Listenable

Plenty of people never touch the games themselves and consume them through recaps, hint posts and daily explainers. That audience includes people who would rather listen than read: commuters, people with low vision, people with dyslexia, and people who just prefer audio.

Turning written puzzle content into audio is mostly a matter of writing for the ear rather than the eye:

- **Spell out what the visuals carry.** "Green in position three" instead of pointing at a tile. If a sentence only works next to a picture, rewrite it.
- **Say the letters, do not show them.** Spangram paths need describing in words: "starts bottom-left, runs along the bottom row". A sighted reader gets this from a highlighted grid; a listener needs the sentence.
- **Warn before the answer.** A listener cannot skim past a spoiler the way a reader skips a paragraph. Announce it and leave a gap.
- **Keep the grid out of the audio.** Reading 48 letters aloud helps nobody. Describe the theme and the shape instead.

For producing the audio itself, text-to-speech has become good enough that a daily format no longer needs a recording session. Tools like [AnySpeech](https://anyspeech.io/) turn written copy into spoken audio, which suits daily recaps well: the script changes every day, and re-recording a voiceover every morning is what usually kills the habit.

The same spoiler discipline applies here as everywhere else on a puzzle site. Audio is harder to skim, so a spoiler in an audio recap is less forgiving than one in text.

## Small Things That Help

If you write anything about daily puzzles, a few habits cost nothing:

1. **Never encode meaning in colour alone.** Say "yellow, the most literal group" rather than relying on the reader seeing yellow.
2. **Give images real alt text.** "Wordle grid" is useless. "Four guesses, answer found on the fourth" is the actual content.
3. **Write positions in words.** Row and column beat "here" and "there" every time.
4. **Mention high contrast mode.** Many players do not know it exists, including plenty who would benefit.

None of this makes a site worse for players who do not need it. Describing a Spangram's path in words is clearer for everyone, and it is the thing a search engine can read too.
