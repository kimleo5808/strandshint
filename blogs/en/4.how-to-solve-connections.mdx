## Connections Is a Game of Restraint

Most people lose NYT Connections not because they can't see the groups, but because they guess too early. You're given 16 words and asked to sort them into four groups of four, with only four mistakes allowed before the game ends. The puzzle is designed so that several words *look* like they belong together when they actually don't. Winning is less about vocabulary and more about discipline: proving a group to yourself before you commit to it.

This guide walks through a repeatable method you can apply to any puzzle, plus the specific traps the editors lean on again and again.

## Understand What the Colors Actually Mean

Each solved group is assigned a color by difficulty:

- **Yellow** — the easiest, most literal connection.
- **Green** — straightforward, but with one small twist.
- **Blue** — harder; often a category you have to *name* precisely.
- **Purple** — the hardest, almost always wordplay: hidden words, homophones, "___ + word," or words that become something else.

Here's the part most players miss: the colors aren't just a scoreboard, they're a hint. If you've found three groups and the leftover four words feel random, they're almost certainly the Purple group — and Purple is rarely about meaning. Stop thinking about what the words *mean* and start thinking about what they *do* (sound like, contain, or precede).

## Start From Certainty, Not From Easiness

The instinct is to grab the group that looks easiest. The better instinct is to grab the group you can **prove** has exactly four members — no more, no fewer.

A category that seems easy but has five plausible candidates is a trap. If you can think of five "types of dog" but the puzzle only wants four, one of those five belongs to a different group, and the editors put it there on purpose. Hold off. Find a group where exactly four words fit and nothing else competes for a slot. That's your safest first guess.

> Rule of thumb: if you can name a fifth candidate for a category, you don't understand that category yet.

## Hunt for the Red Herrings on Purpose

Overlap is the entire game. The editors deliberately include words that fit two categories so you'll place them in the wrong one. Train yourself to look for them:

1. Scan for any word that could plausibly join two groups.
2. Assume it belongs to the **harder** of the two.
3. Build the easier group *without* it and see if four clean members remain.

If the easier group still has four members without the ambiguous word, you've just defused the trap. This single habit prevents most "I was so close" losses.

## Use the "Leftover Four" Check Before You Submit

Before locking in any group, do a quick subtraction. If this group is correct, do the remaining words still split cleanly into sensible groups? If removing your four leaves an obvious orphan — a word that now fits nowhere — your group is probably wrong. The four groups have to work *as a set*, not one at a time.

This is the mental move that separates consistent winners from streak-breakers. You're not solving one group; you're solving a partition of all sixteen.

## Cracking the Purple Group

When you're stuck on Purple, run through the editors' favorite mechanisms:

- **Hidden words:** each word contains a smaller word (e.g. body parts inside other words — sHINg, eARn).
- **Add a letter or word:** every answer becomes a new word or phrase when you add the same thing to each — for example "BOARD" turns CARD, KEY, SURF and BILL into compound words.
- **Homophones:** the words sound like a set even though they're spelled differently.
- **Drop or change a letter:** the answer is one edit away from a themed word.

If the meanings of the four leftover words seem unrelated, that's your signal it's a *form* trick, not a *meaning* trick. Read them out loud — homophone groups often reveal themselves the moment you hear them.

## Manage Your Four Guesses Like a Budget

You have four mistakes. Treat them as a resource, not a safety net:

- Never submit a group you only feel 75% sure about while a 100% group is available.
- If two groups are equally certain, guess the one with **fewer red-herring candidates** first — it's less likely to be the trap.
- Once you've locked two groups, the remaining eight words split into the last two. Guess the one you're surer of; protect your last mistake for the genuinely ambiguous split.

## A Repeatable 5-Step Routine

1. **Read all 16 words first.** Don't react to the first pattern you see.
2. **List candidates for every category you can name** — including the over-full ones.
3. **Find the group with exactly four locked members** and no fifth candidate. Guess it.
4. **Re-check the leftover words** split cleanly. Adjust before guessing again.
5. **Save Purple for last** and switch from meaning to wordplay.

## Practice on Today's Puzzle

The fastest way to internalize this is to apply it daily. Try it on [today's Connections hints](/connections-hint-today), where you can reveal one clue at a time instead of jumping straight to the answer, or work backward through past puzzles in the [Connections archive](/connections-hint) to see how the editors recycle their favorite traps. The patterns repeat — once you've seen twenty Purple groups, the twenty-first feels familiar.

The goal isn't to never be fooled. It's to be fooled *on purpose*, spot the trap, and place the word where the editor hoped you wouldn't.
