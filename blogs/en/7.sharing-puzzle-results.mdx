Wordle did not grow because of marketing. It grew because of a wall of coloured squares.

In late 2021 people started posting a five-by-six grid of green, yellow and grey blocks with a number attached. No words, no screenshots, no explanation. If you did not play, it was noise. If you did, you could read someone's entire game from it: where they got lucky, where they stalled, whether they nearly lost.

That format is worth understanding, because it solved a problem every puzzle game has and most never crack.

## The Problem With Sharing a Puzzle

A daily puzzle has one answer, and everyone gets the same one. That makes it social by nature, and it also makes it impossible to talk about without ruining it for someone.

Post a screenshot and you have spoiled it. Describe your solve and you have spoiled it. Say nothing and the social part disappears entirely.

Josh Wardle's answer was to share the *shape* of the game rather than its content. The grid shows how many guesses you took and how the colours landed, and none of that tells anyone what the word was. Two people can compare a hard day in detail without either learning the answer.

## Why the Squares Read So Well

A few things make the emoji grid work better than it has any right to:

- **It is spoiler-proof by construction.** The information that would ruin the puzzle is simply not in the image.
- **It is legible at a glance.** A row of green means someone nailed it. A grid of grey with a late green run means they struggled and recovered.
- **It travels anywhere.** Emoji are text. They survive being pasted into a group chat, a tweet, a work Slack, an SMS, with no image hosting and no app.
- **It is recognisable to people who do not play.** The shape became a signal in itself, which is how people found the game.

That last point is the one most imitators miss. The grid was not just a result, it was an advertisement that cost nothing to distribute.

## How Strands and Connections Adapted It

Both games inherited the format and had to bend it, because neither has Wordle's clean six-guess structure.

**Connections** shares a four-by-N grid of coloured squares, one row per guess, in the yellow-green-blue-purple difficulty colours. You can see which groups someone found, in what order, and how many mistakes they made. A row of mixed colours is a wrong guess, and four matching squares is a solved category. It reads almost as well as Wordle's, and it carries more information: the order tells you whether someone started from the easy end or dived at purple.

**Strands** had the hardest job, because there is no fixed number of guesses and no per-letter feedback. Its share format uses blue circles for theme words, a yellow bulb for a hint used, and a gold circle for the Spangram, in the order you found them. Someone who opens with the Spangram produces a very different string from someone who found it last, and regular players can read the difference.

If you want the full mechanics behind either game, the [Strands beginner's guide](/guides/beginner-guide) and the [Connections solving guide](/blog/how-to-solve-connections) go through them properly.

## When Squares Are Not Enough

The emoji grid is perfect for one thing: posting your own result, today, to people who play.

It stops being enough the moment you are making something *about* the puzzle rather than just reporting your game. Run a puzzle community, write a daily recap, put together a weekly roundup, explain a tricky Spangram to someone who missed it, and a row of coloured blocks does none of that work.

That is a different kind of visual problem:

- A recap post needs a header image that survives being thumbnailed on social
- A tutorial needs a board you can annotate, not a screenshot with your own answers burned in
- A video walkthrough needs a title card and some motion between the talking parts
- A weekly roundup needs seven of everything, on a schedule, without a design budget

Most people solving this are not designers, and the honest answer for a daily format is that hand-making every asset does not survive contact with a daily schedule. Generated visuals have become the practical route: tools like [imgveo](https://imgveo.com/) produce images and short video clips from a text description, which covers the header-image and title-card end of the job without opening a design tool.

Whatever you use, the constraint from the emoji grid still applies, and it is the part people get wrong: **do not put the answer in the picture**. A thumbnail is the one thing that shows up whether or not someone chose to look at it. Spoilering a daily puzzle in a preview image is a fast way to lose the audience you are making the content for.

## Rules Worth Keeping

If you are sharing puzzle content of any kind, the emoji grid encodes four lessons:

1. **Share the shape, not the answer.** Everything that makes a result interesting can be conveyed without the solution.
2. **Assume no context.** The grid works because you do not need a caption to read it.
3. **Make it survive the platform.** Text-based emoji travel everywhere; an image that gets cropped to a square or shrunk to a thumbnail needs to still work at that size.
4. **Respect the clock.** Everyone gets the same puzzle on the same day, so a spoiler is never just about your own game.

The squares were an accident of design that turned out to be a small masterpiece. Anything you build on top of them is worth holding to the same standard.
