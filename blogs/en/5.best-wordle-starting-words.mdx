## Your First Guess Is the Only One You Can Plan

Every Wordle game starts from the same blank board, which means your opening word is the one decision you can make perfectly *before* the puzzle fights back. A good opener doesn't try to guess the answer — it tries to **learn the most** about it. Get that idea straight and the "best starting word" debate suddenly makes sense.

## What Actually Makes an Opener Good

Three things, in order of importance:

1. **Common letters.** English answers lean heavily on E, A, R, O, T, L, I, S, N. An opener built from these is far more likely to light up green or yellow tiles than one full of rare letters.
2. **Five different letters.** A word like LEVEL wastes a tile testing the same letter twice. On your first guess you want five separate tests, not four.
3. **Letters in realistic positions.** S is common, but it's far more often the *last* letter of an answer than the first. Good openers put letters where they tend to actually appear.

Notice what's *not* on the list: vowels for their own sake. Which brings us to the most overrated opener of all.

## Why ADIEU Is Overrated

ADIEU covers four vowels (A, I, E, U), and that feels efficient. But Wordle answers usually contain only one or two vowels, so a four-vowel opener spends most of its tiles confirming letters that aren't there. Worse, it tests almost no consonants — and consonants are what actually pin down a word. AUDIO has the same problem.

Vowels tell you the shape of a word; consonants tell you *which* word. The best openers balance both.

## Strong Openers, and Why They Work

- **CRANE** — C, R, A, N, E. Five high-frequency letters, all in believable spots. A long-time favorite of solver tools for good reason.
- **SLATE** — S, L, A, T, E. Excellent letter coverage and a very common -ATE ending pattern.
- **CRATE / TRACE / REACT** — same five letters as each other (C, R, A, T, E), just reordered. All elite.
- **SLANT** — no E, which is useful if you want your opener and second guess to cover *different* common letters (more on that below).
- **ROAST** — strong if you like leading with two vowels and the very common R, S, T.

There's no single "correct" answer here. Any word using five common, distinct letters in plausible positions is within a hair of optimal. Pick one you can remember and play it every day — consistency lets you learn how the game responds.

## The Real Edge: Plan Your First Two Words Together

Most of your information comes from the first two guesses combined. The trick is to make them **cover ten different common letters** instead of overlapping.

A clean pairing:

- **Guess 1:** CRANE (C, R, A, N, E)
- **Guess 2:** SLOTH or PILOT (no repeats from guess 1: S, L, O, T, H / P, I, L, O, T)

By the end of guess two you've tested ten of the most common letters in the language. Even with mostly gray tiles, you've eliminated a huge chunk of the dictionary and the answer usually snaps into focus on guess three.

This "two-word opening" works best in the early game when you're gathering information. The moment a green tile appears in a high-value spot, switch modes and start *solving* instead of *exploring*.

## When to Stop Exploring

Use fixed openers only while you're learning the board. Once you have two or three confirmed letters, your next guess should try to **use what you know**, not test new letters blindly:

- Move yellow letters to new positions — yellow means "right letter, wrong spot."
- Never reuse a gray letter; it wastes a full turn.
- Watch for repeated letters in the answer. Players assume five unique letters and miss words like ABBEY or KAYAK. If you've placed four letters and nothing fits, a double is often the reason.

## The Mindset That Wins

Wordle rewards information, not bravado. A guess that can't win but eliminates 800 possibilities is usually better than a long-shot guess at the answer. Open with a strong, common-letter word; pair it with a second word that covers fresh letters; then switch from exploring to solving the instant the board gives you something to work with.
